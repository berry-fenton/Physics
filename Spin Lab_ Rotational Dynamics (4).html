Rotational dynamics for AP Physics 1

Describing rotation

Turn on a ceiling fan. The blade tips race around while the part near the motor barely moves, yet every piece of the blade finishes a turn at the same moment. That’s why we describe spinning with angles instead of distances.

Angular position and displacement, θ

Pick a line painted on the object (the purple line in the simulations). Its angle from a reference direction is the angular position θ. How far it turns is the angular displacement Δθ.

Physics measures angles in radians. One radian is the angle where the arc length equals the radius, so a point at distance r from the axis travels an arc

s = r Δθ   and   1 revolution = 2π rad = 360°This formula only works with θ in radians. Set your calculator accordingly when you use sin for torque, but keep rotation angles in radians.

Angular velocity, ω

How fast the angle changes: ω = Δθ/Δt, in rad/s. Every point on a rigid object shares the same ω.

In real life

A washing machine’s spin cycle runs around 1200 rpm. Convert: 1200 rev/min × 2π rad/rev ÷ 60 s/min ≈ 126 rad/s. That’s what flings water out of your clothes.

Angular acceleration, α

How fast ω changes: α = Δω/Δt, in rad/s². When you switch the fan on, α points the same way as ω and it speeds up. Switch it off and air drag gives an α opposite ω, so it slows.

Signs. AP problems usually call counterclockwise positive and clockwise negative. Pick a direction and stick to it, just like choosing +x in a force problem. This connects to the right-hand rule you already know: curl your fingers the way the object spins and your thumb points along the axis in the direction of ω.

On a carousel, the horses on the outer ring and inner ring complete a lap together (same ω), but the outer horses cover more ground, so they move faster. Kids who want the thrill ride the outside.

Reading graphs

AP loves graphs, and the rules carry over too. The slope of a θ–t graph is ω. The slope of an ω–t graph is α. The area under an ω–t graph is Δθ. The Simulator tab draws all three live.

Rotational inertia

Mass tells you how hard it is to change an object’s straight-line motion. Rotational inertia, I, tells you how hard it is to change its spin. The twist: it depends on where the mass sits, not just how much there is.

Point masses: I = mr²

For a small mass m at distance r from the rotation axis, I = mr². For a collection of small masses, add them up:

I = Σ miri²     units: kg·m²r is measured from the axis of rotation, so the same object has a different I about a different axis.

Why r squared? Two reasons stack. A mass farther out has to reach a bigger speed (v = rω) for the same spin, so it needs more force. And that force is applied over a longer lever arm, which takes more torque. One factor of r from each.

In real life

Choking up on a bat. Sliding your hands up moves the axis of your swing closer to the bat’s heavy barrel. Less mass far from your hands means smaller I, so you can swing it around faster.

The tightrope walker’s pole. A long pole puts mass far from the walker, giving a huge I. When she starts to tip, she rotates slowly, which buys her time to correct.

Spinning in an office chair. Pull your arms and legs in and you speed up; stick them out and you slow down. You’re changing your I mid-spin. (You’ll study why the speed changes in the angular momentum unit.)

Common shapes

Real objects are made of countless tiny masses, so their I is a sum worked out with calculus. You won’t derive these on the AP exam; problems give you the formula. What you need is the pattern: the more mass near the rim, the bigger the number in front of MR².

Net torque makes α

You already know how to find each torque with rF sin θ and add them up with signs. Now for the payoff: what the net torque actually does.

Newton’s second law for rotation

A net force causes linear acceleration: a = Fnet/m. A net torque causes angular acceleration, and rotational inertia plays the role of mass:

α = τnet / I      or      τnet = Iαα always points in the same direction as τnet. Not necessarily the same direction as ω.

Zero net torque does not mean “not spinning”

If τnet = 0, then α = 0, so ω stays constant. Constant could mean at rest, or it could mean spinning at a steady rate forever. This is the rotational version of Newton’s first law, and it shows up on the AP exam constantly.

In real life

A ceiling fan at full speed has zero net torque. The motor’s torque exactly balances the torque from air drag. Nothing is speeding up, but it’s definitely spinning.

A bike wheel spun on a repair stand gradually slows down. The only unbalanced torque is friction in the axle, which points opposite ω, so α is opposite ω.

Car brakes clamp pads onto a disc attached to the wheel. Friction from the pads acts at a distance from the axle, producing a torque opposite the wheel’s spin.

A strategy that always works

  1. Draw the object and mark the axis. Show each force where it acts, the way you’d draw an FBD but with locations.
  2. Choose a positive direction (usually counterclockwise).
  3. Find each torque with rF sin θ and give it a sign. Forces acting at the axis, or pointing straight through it, contribute zero.
  4. Add them to get τnet, then divide by I to get α.
  5. If you need how fast or how far, switch to the rotational kinematics from lesson 1.

A wheel is spinning clockwise at 5 rad/s. Two people then apply torques of 3 N·m counterclockwise and 3 N·m clockwise. What happens over the next few seconds?

Strings and pulleys

Many AP problems link a spinning object to one moving in a straight line: a bucket on a rope wound around a drum, a block hanging from a pulley that has mass. You’ll use both versions of Newton’s second law at once.

In real life

An old well lowers its bucket by unwinding rope from a crank drum. A fishing reel spins as the line pulls out. Elevator cables wrap around a heavy hoist drum. In each, the rope’s tension pulls the hanging object up and twists the drum at the same time.

Two objects, two laws

The hanging mass only translates. Draw its FBD (you’re good at this): weight mg down, tension T up. Taking down as positive:

mg − T = ma

The drum only rotates. Gravity and the axle’s push act at the axis, so they make no torque. The rope pulls tangentially at the rim, so its torque is TR:

TR = Iα

The link. If the rope doesn’t slip, the rim and the rope move together, so the hanging mass’s acceleration equals the rim’s tangential acceleration:

a = Rα

Three equations, three unknowns (a, T, α). Substitute α = a/R into the drum equation to get T = Ia/R², then plug into the mass equation:

a = mg / (m + I/R²)For a solid-cylinder drum, I/R² = ½M, so a = mg / (m + ½M).

Read it like a sentence: gravity on the bucket drives the system, and both the bucket’s mass and the drum’s rotational inertia resist. A massless pulley (I = 0) gives a = g, free fall. A heavy drum slows everything down.

Common traps

  • Tension equals mg only if the hanging mass isn’t accelerating. While it falls, T < mg.
  • Use the drum’s radius for the torque, and the drum’s own I, not the hanging mass.
  • Keep signs consistent: the direction the mass moves should match the direction the drum turns.
