Investigation guide

Your goal: figure out how ionic and covalent (molecular) compounds differ in their physical properties, and explain why using what you know about their particles and the forces between them.

Step 1: Identify before you test

When you choose a compound, the tests are locked until you correctly identify its bonding from its formula. Use the periodic table: does the formula contain a metal? For covalent compounds, think about electronegativity and the molecule's shape.

Part 1: Collect data (Explore mode)

  1. Test at least 4 ionic and 4 covalent compounds at every station. Your results go into the lab notebook automatically.
  2. At the conductivity station, test each compound three ways: as the pure sample, dissolved in water, and melted (molten).
  3. At the solubility station, try both water (polar) and hexane (nonpolar).

Part 2: Look for trends (Trends tab)

  1. Is there a melting-point "dividing line" that separates most ionic compounds from most covalent ones? Drag the slider to find the best one. Are there any exceptions?
  2. Do all ionic compounds dissolve in water? Find at least one that doesn't. What happens to its conductivity in water?
  3. Does any ionic compound conduct electricity as a solid? What has to happen to the ions before the bulb lights up?
  4. Which compounds dissolve in hexane? What do they have in common? (Hint: "like dissolves like.")
  5. Compare the crush test results. How does the arrangement of ions in a crystal explain what you see?

Part 3: Explain it (claim, evidence, reasoning)

  • Claim: State one property that reliably tells ionic and covalent compounds apart.
  • Evidence: Cite specific measurements from your notebook (at least three compounds).
  • Reasoning: Connect the evidence to particles: ions and electrostatic attraction in a lattice vs. neutral molecules held by intermolecular forces.

Part 4: Mystery samples

Switch to Mystery samples. Test the unknowns, classify each one in the notebook as ionic or covalent, then press Check my classifications. Be ready to defend each choice with at least two pieces of evidence.

Challenge questions (accelerated)

  • Hydrogen chloride (HCl) is a covalent molecule, yet its water solution makes the bulb glow brightly. Pure HCl does not. What happens to HCl molecules in water?
  • Benzoic acid and acetic acid make the bulb glow only dimly. Why would a solution conduct "a little"?
  • Sodium nitrate melts at about 308 °C and iodine melts at 114 °C. Why is melting point alone not enough to classify an unknown?
  • Turn on network solids. Quartz (SiO₂) and diamond are covalent, but they melt at extremely high temperatures. How is their structure different from a molecular solid like sugar?

Teacher settings

Every attempt is counted. The notebook shows how many tries each identification took and how many were right on the first try. Changing the level relocks all compounds. Settings are saved in this browser, and students can see this tab.

Teacher notes

  • Values are based on literature data (melting points in °C at 1 atm, solubility in g per 100 mL water at about 25 °C). Each run adds small random measurement error, as a real lab would, so students' numbers will differ slightly.
  • Conductivity readings are illustrative values in µS/cm. They are scaled to show strong, weak and non-electrolyte behavior clearly, not to match a particular concentration.
  • If you turn the identification step off, a Show bond types switch appears in the header. Untick it to have students classify compounds in the notebook instead. The notebook is saved in this browser only. Use Copy table to paste the data into a spreadsheet or lab report.
